A memory that organizes itself.

TRACE sorts a growing library into groups from the passages themselves. A question then wakes only a small part of it, and every answer comes with a record you can check.

The idea in three beats

Order, then a small wake-up, then proof.

1. It sorts itself

Measured by us

Passages that mean alike end up together, and no one adds labels. The order comes from the passages.

about 7groups hold a search's ten best matchesAt most 10 are possible; groups made at random would give 10. 50 test searches, our library and method. We measured how ordered the finished groups are, not how they formed.

2. A question wakes a part

Measured by us

A question is compared with the groups first, and only the groups most likely to hold the answer are searched.

0.79%of one of four equal parts is scored per searchAbout 127 times less scoring work, and 0.9985 of exact search's top ten kept. 50 test searches, our library and method, not reproduced on public data.

3. Every answer has a record

Working today

Each result shows which article it came from, where in it, and a fingerprint your browser re-computes.

144real recorded searches you can openOur own re-run matched on all claims. No outside party has checked it. Open the explorer.

Honest limits

What we did not show

  1. Keyword search finds the source more often.

    On our 50 test searches, ordinary keyword search put the right source passage in its top ten for 82% of them. TRACE did for 52%, and exact search by meaning also got 52%, so the gap is meaning versus keywords, not our shortcut.

  2. A plain scan takes less time per search, and our stored size on disk is bigger.

    Per search: keyword 6 ms, exact scan 88 ms, TRACE 1.2 s. Stored size on disk: 2.28 GB for TRACE, 1.25 GB for plain vectors, 0.51 GB for the keyword index. TRACE's saving is scoring work, not time or space.

  3. The headline result is for our own library and method.

    It has not been reproduced on public data. A simple stand-in of ours (not TRACE itself) on three small public sets needed 7% to 28% of passages scored to keep about 95%, and under 1% kept only 52% on the largest.

  4. Scale: not as far as it may sound.

    We missed our 100M target by 0.11%. We have not run a billion tokens. The 1.53 billion item is a separate group-picking test. The 504.5M build is an earlier test by us, on a machine since shut down.

  5. The groups come from an ordinary sorting step.

    On our library it ran once. We measured how ordered the finished groups are (a search's best matches share about 7 groups, and arrival order alone gives about 8), not how they formed.
