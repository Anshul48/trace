Scale: one measured library

Measured result: the sealed library

Working today, our server 99.9Mtokens in the sealed library: 812,545 passages

The full measured pipeline: sorted into groups, searched, every answer with a checkable record, re-run on our own server. This is the library behind every number on this site that is not marked as an earlier test.

Short of our 100M target by 0.11% (108,154 tokens). Our own library and method; not reproduced on public data; no outside party has checked it.

The measurement · Explorer

A token is a word-sized piece of text. The earlier larger tests (310.5M, 504.5M and 1.53B tokens) are kept apart, in the appendix at the end of this page, with all their caveats.

The measured result

Routed retrieval kept 0.9985 of exact results while scoring 0.79% of passages

The library holds 812,545 passages (99,891,846 tokens), sealed in four equal parts of about 203,000 passages. We ran 50 test searches (40 written questions and 10 taken from the openings of passages) against it and compared TRACE with exact search by meaning, which compares the question with every passage.

0.79%of passages scoredon one of four equal parts
~127×less scoring workthan scoring every row
0.9985of exact search's top ten kept1 would mean identical

Reported exact-top-ten retention: 0.9985 across 50 test searches; 0.79% passage scoring measured on one of four equal library parts. This measures preservation of exact vector results, not answer quality or relevance. The 127-fold figure is passage-scoring work only: choosing the groups, building them and the end-to-end time are separate costs (end-to-end, a TRACE search took 1.2 s against 88 ms for an exact scan).

The library is short of our 100M-token target by 0.11% (108,154 tokens). We waived that gate and say so. On the live copy of the library on our server, the checkable records matched on all the claims we re-ran. No outside party has checked any of this.

The measured TRACE search is vector search only, with no keyword part. An older engine in the repository does combine the two, but we have no result for it on this library.

Is the library in order?

The groups hold best matches together, modestly

We asked a plain question: when a search's ten true best matches are found by exact search, how many different groups do they sit in? If the sorting were useless, they would sit in 10 different groups. The library has about 22,995 groups per quarter, around 9 passages each.

First quarter of the library (the one the 0.79% and 0.9985 were measured on), 50 test searches; the other three quarters gave 7.1, 6.8 and 7.1, and 6.9 across all four. The edges are soft: a passage is about as close (0.47) to a member of the next-nearest group as to its own group-mates (0.55).

Plainly: the best matches are held together far more than chance, and somewhat more than the order in which passages happened to arrive. They are not held in one or two groups. TRACE has to look inside many groups to keep 0.9985, and the 0.79% already counts that.

Does the order hold as the collection gets bigger?

We sorted the first N passages of one collection with one recipe, at five sizes, and asked the same question. The answer, at every size we tested, is the same: a band between 4.7 and 5.0 groups, with no trend either way.

What this does not show

  • Nothing here shows the memory organising itself as a process. The groups come from one ordinary sorting run (a standard method, k-means, five passes, once per quarter). We measured how ordered the finished result is, not how it formed.
  • We have no before-and-after curve of one library as it grows. A controlled check sorted the same collection at five sizes from 10,000 to 200,000 passages with one recipe: a passage's ten closest matches fell in 4.7 to 5.0 groups at every size (random groups: 10). So the order is present at every size and neither builds up nor collapses as the set grows. We do not claim the order improves with scale, and the margin over arrival order did not widen. The largest set we sorted and measured holds 233,000 passages; we make no claim about millions or billions.
  • We did not show that the order is why sparse search works inside TRACE's own engine. A simplified stand-in (not TRACE) was consistent with it: see the control below, where cutting the same passages into random groups made the stand-in fail.
  • The 50 test searches were chosen earlier for other tests. They are not a random sample of possible searches, and the library is ours.

A control: the grouping switched off

Cut the same passages into random groups, and the shortcut mostly stops working

If the grouping did nothing, searching a small share of it at random groups would work as well. We tested that on the first quarter of the library (203,137 passages, 22,995 groups). We used 500 stored passages as the questions, each looking for its own ten closest passages (found by exact search). A simple router compared the question with the centre of every group, opened the nearest groups first, and stopped when a set share of the passages had been scored. We then asked how many of the true ten were inside what it scored. We did the same with the same group sizes cut three other ways.

Read this label first

Passages as questions, which stand in for real searches. A simplified nearest-group router, not TRACE’s own guided router, and not the engine that produced the 0.9985. The cost of choosing the groups is not counted in “passages scored”, and no timings were taken. Our grouping, not checked by anyone outside our team.

A second library, the 200,000-passage collection from the size check above with its own sorting run, gave the same picture: 98.2%, 28.8% and 87.2% at 0.5% scored. With 500 passages held out of the library as the questions, the closer stand-in for a new question, it gave 95.9%, 21.7% and 83.1%.

What this shows, and what it does not

  • The grouping matters. At 1% of passages scored, our groups found 99.0% of the true ten. Random groups of the same sizes found 36.9%.
  • Part of the help is the order the passages arrive in. Passages arrive in document order, so blocks of consecutive passages already found 87.3% at 0.5%. This is a hard control, not a “no organization” control: the random column is the true “off” arm. Our grouping adds the rest: to find 90%, it scored 0.09% of the passages against 0.86% for blocks, about a tenth.
  • Random groups are not at zero. At 0.5% scored, picking passages at random would find 0.5%. Random groups found 28.5%, probably because the centre of about nine random passages still leans toward each of them. Weak, not nothing.
  • The cost of choosing the groups is not in these numbers. This router compared each question with all 22,995 group centres, which is 11.3% of the quarter’s passage count in dot products. That is the same for all three columns, so the comparison between them stands, but the absolute saving does not. A router that looks in two steps would cost less, and we did not measure it.
  • This is not TRACE’s own router. The groups come from ordinary k-means, and we put each passage in the group with the nearest centre in the same space the router uses, which favours our groups with this router. Nothing here separates a contribution of TRACE from ordinary k-means grouping. We did not run guided routing against ordinary k-means with a standard group count, or against other search indexes.

What we do not say

  • That random groups “do nothing” (they found 28.5% at 0.5%).
  • That this is faster, or any speed-up: the cost of choosing the groups and the time were not measured.
  • That TRACE’s own algorithm, rather than ordinary k-means grouping, causes this.
  • That it holds for real questions, or for the full 812,545-passage library as one memory.
  • Anything about other search indexes.

Raw outputs and the script: receipts/order-measure in the repository.

What a skeptic asks

The hard questions, answered plainly

Does it find the right source?

Not as often as keyword search. On our 50 test searches, ordinary keyword search put the right source in its top ten for 82% of them; TRACE and exact search by meaning each reached 52%. Our 0.9985 measures how much of exact vector search's answer TRACE kept, not answer quality.

Does a matching fingerprint prove nothing relevant was skipped?

No. A matching fingerprint proves the passage you were shown is the one that was stored, with its article and position. Whether the shortcut skipped something relevant is a different question, and the comparison with exact search measures it: 0.9985 of exact search's top ten kept.

Is 0.79% the whole cost?

No. It counts passage-scoring work only. Choosing the groups, building them and the end-to-end time are separate costs: a TRACE search took 1.2 s against 88 ms for an exact scan, and keyword search took 6 ms.

Is the saving from self-organization, or from ordinary grouping?

We have not separated the two inside TRACE's own engine. The groups come from an ordinary sorting step, and we measured how ordered they are, not how they formed. In a simplified stand-in (not TRACE's own router; the cost of choosing the groups is not counted), scoring 0.5% of a quarter of the library found 98.1% of a passage's ten true closest matches through our groups, 87.3% through blocks of passages in arrival order, and 28.5% through random groups of the same sizes. So the grouping matters, and part of the help is the order the passages arrive in. The order we measured shows up at every size we tested (10,000 to 200,000 passages, a flat band with no trend), and we do not claim it improves with scale. The control, with its caveats.

Does it work on public data?

TRACE has not been run there. A stand-in of ours gave dataset-specific results: FiQA about 95% at 7% scored, SciFact about 95% at 24%, NFCorpus 90% at 28% and 97% at 55%.

Has anyone outside your team checked it?

No. Outside verification is pending. What you can check yourself is on Verify.

Honest limits

What we did not show

  1. Ordinary keyword search finds the source more often.

    On our 50 test searches, ordinary keyword search put the right source passage in its top ten for 82% of them. TRACE did for 52%, and exact search by meaning also got 52%, so the gap is meaning versus keywords, not our shortcut. On a stricter score that also rewards putting the source first, keyword search got 0.74 and TRACE 0.42. TRACE is for search by meaning that you can check, not a replacement for keyword search.

  2. A plain scan takes less time per search, and our stored size on disk is bigger.

    Per search: keyword 6 ms, exact scan 88 ms, TRACE 1.2 s. Stored size on disk: 2.28 GB for TRACE, 1.25 GB for plain vectors, 0.51 GB for the keyword index. Stored size on disk; memory use was not compared. TRACE's saving is passage-scoring work. We make no claim about speed or space.

  3. We have no like-for-like measure of memory in use.

    The one memory figure we measured is the live service's peak, 1.03 GiB, while answering the 50 real test questions. We did not measure the other methods the same way, so we compare nothing.

  4. The headline result is for our own library and method.

    TRACE itself has not been run on public benchmarks. A simple stand-in of ours (not TRACE) that groups passages with ordinary k-means gave dataset-specific results on three small public sets (3,600 to 57,600 passages): FiQA kept about 95% of exact search's top ten at 7% of passages scored, SciFact about 95% at 24%, and NFCorpus 90% at 28% and 97% at 55%. Scoring under 1% (FiQA, one group) kept only 52%. So 0.79% at 0.9985 is not reproduced on public data, and it must not be read as a general property of grouping.

  5. Scale: not as far as it may sound.

    We missed our 100M target by 0.11%. The 504.5M and 310.5M builds are earlier tests by us, on a cloud machine since shut down, so nobody can re-run them; they are in a separate appendix. The 1.53 billion item is a separate group-picking test. We have not run a billion tokens through TRACE.

  6. The groups come from an ordinary sorting step.

    One run per quarter. We measured how ordered the finished groups are (above), not how they formed, and we have no growth curve: the order was present at every size we sorted, up to 233,000 passages, and did not clearly improve with size. The sorting takes real time up front.

  7. Nobody outside our team has checked these results.

    The numbers come from our own runs. The checkable records and the code check let you verify the parts that can be verified, and the rest is our word until someone re-runs it.

  8. There is no live public service, and no download of the whole library yet.

    A public backend was switched off after it failed its first test with many users. The explorer replays 144 real recorded searches. A download for your own computer is planned, not built.

Corrections

Numbers we corrected before publishing

  • Stored size, not memory. An earlier draft called 2.28 GB, 1.25 GB and 0.51 GB “serving memory”. They are stored size on disk, and this site says so. The only memory figure we measured is the live service's 1.03 GiB peak.
  • 82% and 52% are shares of searches. An earlier draft presented them as a rank-aware score. They are the share of the 50 test searches that put the source in the top ten, and the stricter score is in the first limit above.
  • Public benchmarks. TRACE has not been run on them. The small stand-in run is reported above, including that it did not reach our headline.

The 504.5M and 310.5M builds, with every caveat

Read this first

These are earlier tests by us, from late October 2026, on a cloud machine that has since been shut down. They cannot be re-run. The records are ours, are not verified by anyone outside, and the underlying machine files are gone. They are not the sealed library, and none of their numbers belongs next to the 0.79% and 0.9985 above.

504,480,146 tokens, sealed in 13 pieces (7.65 GB stored size on disk; memory use was not compared)

  • Exact search matched the ground truth: 1.0, no errors.
  • With one piece switched off, it still found 0.885 of the exact results, with no wrong passages returned. The missing share is the part that lived on the switched-off piece.
  • The compressed-search figure (0.994 to 0.998 of the exact top ten) was measured only on the five newest pieces, never on all 13.
  • A search across 13 pieces took a typical 6.86 s, with pieces searched one at a time. That is slow, and it is not served at that size.
  • Record: RECEIPT-RUNG-500M.txt. It was written by one of our automated workers, not by a person, and the underlying machine files are gone.

310,532,353 tokens, built in 8 pieces

  • Exact search matched the ground truth. With one piece switched off it found 0.87 of the exact results, with no wrong passages, and a deliberately planted fault was detected, as it had to be.
  • The compressed-search figure was 0.995 to 0.998 on all 8 pieces.
  • A search took a typical 4.18 s, pieces searched one at a time.
  • Record: RECEIPT-RUNG-300M.txt.

“Found 0.885 (or 0.87) of the exact results” means: of the true top ten that exact search returns, that share came back, with the missing ones simply absent. It is not a measure of answer quality.

1.53 billion tokens: group-picking only

On 16 October 2026, on rented cloud graphics cards, we encoded 1,531,809,092 tokens of a different, larger collection into 7,134,906 searchable vectors, in 109 pieces. A routed search over those vectors, looking only at the most likely groups, kept 0.9947 of the exact top ten while looking at 6.6% of the vectors (1,024 groups, 64 looked at, 5,000 held-out test queries).

What was not done

  • The vectors were not merged into one index and were not served through TRACE.
  • There were no checkable records at that size.
  • There was no run with a billion vectors, and we have not run a billion tokens through TRACE.
  • Its numbers must never be read together with the sealed library's: it is a different collection with groups hundreds of times larger.

Cost to build, at a small size

This is the one build-cost comparison we have, and it is small: a test on a smaller collection, on the same machine. One run peaked at 22.0 GiB of memory and took 12,973 s. A later run peaked at 8.3 GiB and took 9,495 s.

Read before using

  • One run each. The later run was not like-for-like: it picked candidates with an approximate method and stored data in columns. Its find rate was 0.7367 against 0.7300, and it used about 6% more disk.
  • This is building the memory, not serving searches. The first run's record is in the repository (receipts/result.json); the second run's record is not published yet.
  • It was a test on a smaller collection. We did not measure it at larger sizes.
