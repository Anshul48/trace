A memory that organizes itself.

Self-organizing memory prototype. Demonstrated result: routed vector retrieval kept 0.9985 of exact top-ten results while scoring 0.79% of passages (50 test searches, one of four equal library parts). Every answer comes with a record you can check.

The idea in three beats

Order, then a small wake-up, then proof.

1. It sorts itself

{{badge:groups-hold-matches}}

Passages that mean alike end up together, and no one adds labels. The order comes from the passages.

about 7groups hold a search's ten best matchesAt most 10 are possible; groups made at random would give 10. 50 test searches, our library and method. We measured how ordered the finished groups are, not how they formed.

2. A question wakes a part

{{badge:sparse-search}}

A question is compared with the groups first, and only the groups most likely to hold the answer are searched.

0.79%of one of four equal parts is scored per searchAbout 127 times less passage-scoring work (routing and building the groups are separate costs), and 0.9985 of exact search's top ten kept. 50 test searches, our library and method, not reproduced on public data.

3. Every answer has a record

{{badge:record-check}}

Each result shows which article it came from, where in it, and a fingerprint your browser re-computes.

144real recorded searches you can openOur own re-run matched on all claims. No outside party has checked it. Open the explorer.

Honest limits

What we did not show

  1. Keyword search finds the source more often.

    On our 50 test searches, ordinary keyword search put the right source passage in its top ten for 82% of them. TRACE did for 52%, and exact search by meaning also got 52%, so the gap is meaning versus keywords, not our shortcut.

  2. A plain scan takes less time per search, and our stored size on disk is bigger.

    Per search: keyword 6 ms, exact scan 88 ms, TRACE 1.2 s. Stored size on disk: 2.28 GB for TRACE, 1.25 GB for plain vectors, 0.51 GB for the keyword index. Stored size on disk; memory use was not compared. TRACE's saving is passage-scoring work, not time or space.

  3. The headline result is for our own library and method.

    TRACE itself has not been run on public data. A simple stand-in of ours (not TRACE) on three small public sets gave dataset-specific results: FiQA kept about 95% of exact search's top ten at 7% of passages scored, SciFact about 95% at 24%, and NFCorpus 90% at 28% and 97% at 55%. Scoring under 1% (FiQA, one group) kept only 52%.

  4. Scale: not as far as it may sound.

    We missed our 100M target by 0.11%. We have not run a billion tokens. The 1.53 billion item is a separate group-picking test. The 504.5M and 310.5M builds are earlier tests by us, on a machine since shut down, shown separately above.

  5. The groups come from an ordinary sorting step.

    On our library it ran once. We measured how ordered the finished groups are (a search's best matches share about 7 groups, and arrival order alone gives about 8), not how they formed. The order did not clearly grow with size in our small checks, and we make no claim at millions or billions.
