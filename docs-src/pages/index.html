A memory that organizes itself.

Self-organizing memory prototype. Demonstrated result: routed vector retrieval kept 0.9985 of exact top-ten results while scoring 0.79% of passages (50 test searches, one of four equal library parts). Every answer comes with a record you can check.

In plain words: imagine a library where nobody files the books. TRACE puts the shelves in order by itself, so passages that mean alike end up together. Ask a question and it walks to a few shelves instead of reading every book, then shows the page each answer came from, with a code you can check.

Why this matters now: if you use an AI agent every day, you know it forgets. TRACE is an early form of the memory such an agent needs: one it can search and check instead of starting over. What works today is the recorded replay one click below.

The idea in three beats

Nobody files the books. A question walks to a few shelves. Every answer shows its page.

1. It sorts itself

{{badge:groups-hold-matches}}

Passages that mean alike end up on the same shelf, and no one adds labels. The order comes from the passages.

about 7shelves hold a search's ten best matchesAt most 10 are possible; groups made at random would give 10. 50 test searches, our library and method. We measured how ordered the finished groups are, not how they formed.

2. A question wakes a part

{{badge:sparse-search}}

A question is compared with the shelves first, and only the shelves most likely to hold the answer are searched.

0.79%of one of four equal parts is scored per searchAbout 127 times less passage-scoring work (choosing the shelves and building them are separate costs), and 0.9985 of exact search's top ten kept. 50 test searches, our library and method.

3. Every answer has a record

{{badge:record-check}}

Each result shows which article it came from, where in it, and a fingerprint your browser re-computes.

Honest limits

What a skeptic asks

  1. Does it find the right source?

    Not as often as ordinary keyword search: on our 50 test searches the right source was in its top ten for 82% of them, against 52% for TRACE (exact search by meaning also got 52%). The 0.9985 measures how much of exact search's answer was kept, not answer quality.

  2. Is it faster or smaller?

    No. A TRACE search took 1.2 s, a plain scan 88 ms, keyword search 6 ms. Stored size on disk: 2.28 GB for TRACE, 1.25 GB for plain vectors; memory use was not compared. The saving is passage-scoring work only.

  3. Does a matching fingerprint prove nothing relevant was skipped?

    No. It proves the passage shown is the one that was stored. Whether the shortcut skipped something relevant is measured by the comparison with exact search (0.9985 kept).

  4. Is it the self-organizing, or just ordinary grouping?

    We have not separated the two inside TRACE's own engine. In a simplified control, random groups of the same sizes found 28.5% of the true matches where ours found 98.1% (0.5% of passages scored), and arrival order alone found 87.3%. The control and its caveats.

  5. Does it work on public data?

    TRACE has not been run there. A stand-in of ours (not TRACE) gave dataset-specific results: FiQA about 95% of exact search's top ten at 7% of passages scored, SciFact about 95% at 24%, NFCorpus 90% at 28% and 97% at 55%.

  6. Has anyone outside your team checked it?

    No. Outside verification is pending. There is no live public service and no download of the whole library yet; the explorer is a replay of real recorded searches.
